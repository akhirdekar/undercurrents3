About the data

About this project

Where do mapped catch basins and reported catch basin problems overlap, and how do report patterns change around wet periods? The primary audience is civil engineers and environmental planners preparing questions for site visits; community boards and residents can explore the same evidence.

Undercurrents appears here as both hidden infrastructure and the less visible work of noticing, reporting and maintaining it.

How the data was prepared

The report file labels its request numbers UNITID. These were renamed REQUEST_ID to avoid confusion with basin IDs. A report number and a basin ID refer to different things; we do not match them to each other.

Original coordinates and problem descriptions are kept. Dates were recovered by looking up each exact 311 request number. We checked for repeated IDs, missing values and coordinates outside NYC. Full NYC borough outlines were used to place records; the outlines on screen are simplified for faster drawing.

Each hexagon covers 1 km². Areas at the edges may include water or land outside NYC. Counts are not adjusted for land area, road length or population. Borough filters count only records in that borough. Different basin IDs at the same coordinates are kept. The color scale changes with your filters.

About the rainfall

Rain comes from NOAA’s daily Central Park record, station USW00094728. Values are converted from tenths of a millimeter to millimeters. Readings marked with a quality problem, or a missing-value code, are left out. Trace amounts appear as zero and are marked in the chart readout.

Central Park does not measure rain across every borough. Rainfall observation days may differ from the calendar days in 311. Storms, seasons and reporting delays affect comparisons. A wet-day shortcut shows two days before and four days after the reading, so you can look for delayed reports. This does not prove that rain caused a problem.

Daily totals include the water equivalent of snow and other frozen precipitation. They do not show how hard it rained in a short burst. Missing readings remain gaps, rather than becoming zero.

What the data can’t tell us

311 shows what people report, and who can access reporting. It does not capture every drainage problem. Several requests may concern the same place. More drains do not necessarily mean better drainage; more reports do not necessarily mean worse service. Names, contact details and street addresses are not shown.

The basin file does not show condition, how much water an inlet or pipe can carry, repair results, the area draining into it, height or a history of installations. A closed request does not confirm a repair, so closure is not used to judge performance.

“Reports per 100 basins” divides reports in an area by the fixed number of mapped basins, then multiplies by 100. Basin counts stay fixed when dates change. This is not a failure rate: reports are not linked to individual basins. A ratio based on very few basins can be misleading.

The supplied reports are part of the source system, not a confirmed complete record. The original download date and earlier cleaning steps were not provided. Choosing dates does not make the file complete. This educational project uses public data; public availability is not treated as proof of consent.

Possible next steps

A useful next layer is NYC’s official stormwater flood map, with each rainfall scenario and its assumptions clearly labeled. Ground height alone cannot tell us whether a drain is adequate. Slope, paved surfaces, drain shape, pipes and rainfall intensity also matter.

Ground-height data should state its detail, date, map coordinates and how heights are referenced. Measure it consistently and keep ground height separate from water depth. Comparing rainfall across neighborhoods would need several gauges or a documented radar dataset.
